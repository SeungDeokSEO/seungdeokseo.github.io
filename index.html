I am a Ph.D. student in Political Science (Comparative Politics and International Relations) at the University of Illinois Urbana–Champaign. I am currently conducting dissertation fieldwork as a Visiting Researcher at the Center for Strategic Studies, University of Jordan. My research focuses on foreign aid, NGOs, and civil society, particularly in the Middle East and North Africa.

My dissertation, Together in Public, Apart in Practice, examines why civil society organizations (CSOs) participating in the same advocacy campaigns vary in the depth of their cooperation. Focusing on Jordan, I argue that stronger competition over credit for collective outcomes discourages substantive cooperation.

Beyond my dissertation, my research examines how political incentives and institutions shape foreign aid and civil society. My current projects investigate how local political interests influence aid allocation, how donor identity shapes public opinion toward foreign aid, and how restrictive NGO laws affect civil society activity.

My research has received support from the Institute for Humane Studies, the University of Illinois Graduate College, the Department of Political Science, and the Evelyne Accad and Paul Vieille International Research Award.

Research

  • Together in Public, Apart in Practice: Credit Competition and the Depth of Civil Society Cooperation in Jordan (Dissertation Project)
    Abstract

    Why do NGOs that publicly join the same advocacy campaigns sometimes cooperate deeply but at other times remain strategically shallow? I argue that competition over credit for joint outcomes shapes the depth of cooperation. Visible participation allows organizations to associate themselves with a campaign's achievements, while attribution concerns can discourage information sharing and substantive coordination. Focusing on Jordan, the project combines original data on NGO cooperation, survey experiments, and qualitative interviews.

  • Do Cross-National Aid Allocation Patterns Travel Sub-Nationally? Evidence from Saudi Aid Allocation in Indonesia (with Paul Un) — Under review
    Abstract

    The foreign aid literature argues that donors allocate aid according to strategic, political, and identity-based interests. Yet it remains unclear whether such patterns also appear within recipient states. Using project-level data from the Saudi Aid Platform, we examine Saudi aid allocation across subnational units in Indonesia. We find limited evidence that aid is directed toward districts with larger Muslim populations, but more consistent evidence of an association with the legislative seat share of Islamist parties.

  • Whose Aid Is Trusted? Donor Identity and Religious Congruence in Egypt and Indonesia (with Paul Un)
    Abstract

    Why do recipient publics trust some aid donors more than others? We investigate whether perceived religious congruence between donors and recipients shapes donor legitimacy and interpretations of donor motives. The project uses conjoint survey experiments in Egypt and Indonesia that vary donor identity, project sector, and other project characteristics to study evaluations of foreign aid.

  • Staying Registered, Going Quiet: How Restrictive NGO Laws Suppress Civil Society Activity (with Tae Il Kim)
    Abstract

    Restrictive NGO laws have proliferated across aid-receiving countries. Although these laws may not immediately reduce the number of registered NGOs, they can discourage organizations from participating in civil society activities. We examine this potential chilling effect using an updated dataset of restrictive NGO laws and V-Dem measures of civil society participation across aid-receiving countries.
